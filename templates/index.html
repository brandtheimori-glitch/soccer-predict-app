A couple of newer entrants (ScoreGPT, SignalOdds) do better by naming their models and grading every pick — that transparency principle is the main thing this prototype borrows. So the design goals here are: 1. **Show the work.** Every prediction has a "why?" panel with the actual inputs (attack/defense strength vs. league average, recent form, model vs. market probabilities) — never just a bare percentage. 2. **Grade yourself in public.** Every prediction is logged before the match, then automatically graded against the real result. The running accuracy is shown on the page, with the full log visible, not just a summary number. 3. **One quick answer, details on demand.** The default view is a single pick + score + confidence per match (fast to scan, addressing the stat-tool problem above); the underlying stats are one click away (addressing the black-box "AI" problem). 4. **Free-tier friendly.** API-Football's free plan (100 requests/day) is used instead of football-data.org's, whose free tier only exposes a competition list, not actual match data. Live responses are cached for 6 hours so normal browsing doesn't burn through the daily quota. 5. **Find the actual value.** Beyond "who wins," each match is checked for value bets — cases where the model's own probability for an outcome sits meaningfully above what the market's odds imply. That's the professional framing (a mispriced outcome), not just a pick. ## How the model works `model.py` implements a standard approach for goal-based sports prediction (the same family as Maher 1982 / Dixon-Coles): 1. Each team gets an **attack strength** (their average goals scored ÷ the league's overall average goals per team per game) and **defense strength** (average goals conceded ÷ that same league average). 2. Multiplying those together, plus a single league-wide **home-advantage** multiplier applied only to the home side, gives an **expected goals (xG)** figure for each side in a specific fixture. 3. Treating each side's goals as independent Poisson-distributed variables around those xG values produces a full score matrix (0-0, 1-0, 0-1, …), which is summed to get win/draw/loss probabilities, over/under, BTTS, and the most likely scorelines. 4. Where bookmaker odds are available, their de-vigged implied probabilities are blended in (65% model / 35% market by default) — markets price in information (injuries, suspensions, weather) a simple stats model can't see, so blending tends to be better calibrated than either signal alone. 5. Separately, the **model-only** probability (before blending) is compared to the market's implied probability for the same outcome. If the model thinks an outcome is at least 5 points more likely than the market's price implies — and the expected value of betting it at that price is positive — it's flagged as a value bet in the UI, with the actual odds, both probabilities, and the expected value shown. This is a solid, well-understood baseline — it's what most serious prediction systems use as a foundation before adding machine learning on top. It is *not* state-of-the-art (a production system would add player- level data, fatigue/travel, and a model trained on historical outcomes), but it's honest about exactly what's driving each number, which is the main gap identified in the research. ## Fitting home advantage and team strength from real results The first version of this hand-picked a home-advantage constant (1.35, "it felt about right") and applied it on top of a league-wide home/away goal split that was *already* baking in a home-scoring boost — double-counting the effect. `fit_parameters.py` replaces both with numbers estimated from actual match results: - **One pooled league average** (goals per team per game, home and away combined) instead of separate home/away averages. - **Home advantage** fitted as the ratio of average home-team goals to that pooled average, from real results — not assumed. - **Each team's attack/defense** computed the same way, from goals scored and conceded across every match in the fit sample (home and away together), relative to the pooled average. Free-tier data means small samples — a demo league here has 14-18 historical matches to fit from, and even a live API-Football key's free plan limits how many past seasons you can pull. Fitting a ratio from a handful of matches is noisy (one freak scoreline can badly skew a team's rating), so every fitted number is **shrunk toward a neutral prior** in proportion to how little data supports it: teams with few games lean toward league-average attack/defense (1.00), and the league's home advantage leans toward a documented typical value (1.30) until enough matches accumulate to trust the fitted number over the prior. The "why?" panel shows both the raw and shrunk estimate, plus the sample size, so it's never hiding how much to trust a given number. A side effect worth knowing: fitting from raw match results this way meant dropping the per-team `/teams/statistics` API-Football call entirely — `historical_matches()` pulls one batch of recent results per league, which covers every team's fit input from that single call. That's fewer requests against the 100/day free-tier cap than the previous per-team-lookup design. ### Recency weighting A match from three months ago says less about a team's CURRENT strength than one from last week — squads change, form swings, managers get sacked. Every historical match is now weighted by an exponential decay based on its age: `weight = 0.5 ** (days_ago / 100)`, so a match 100 days old counts half as much as one today, 200 days old a quarter, and so on. The 100-day half-life isn't arbitrary — it's the same ballpark Dixon & Coles (1997) fitted from real results (their xi=0.0065/day implies ~107 days). This changes what "sample size" means for the shrinkage described above: it's now an EFFECTIVE sample size (the sum of each match's weight), not a raw count. 18 matches spread over two seasons carry less real information than 18 from the last two months, and the fit now reflects that — the "why?" panel shows both numbers side by side (e.g. "18 historical matches, 14.2 effective after recency weighting"). ## Checking whether value bets actually work, and tuning the threshold Flagging a value bet is a claim ("this price is wrong"), and claims should get checked. `track_record.value_bet_performance()` (surfaced as its own section in the UI and at `/api/value-bet-performance`) does that: 1. Every value bet is logged at prediction time with its odds and both probabilities — never edited after the fact. Alongside it, the model also logs the model-vs-market comparison for ALL THREE outcomes of every match, not just the ones that cleared the threshold at the time (see `model.compute_all_edges()`) — that full record is what makes the threshold sweep below possible. 2. Once a match finishes, each flagged bet (and every logged edge) is graded won/lost, with the unit return (odds − 1 if won, −1 if lost) recorded alongside it. 3. The running total gives a win rate and ROI, compared against the **break-even win rate the odds themselves implied** for those specific bets (average of 1/odds) — that's the honest baseline, not 0%. 4. Because a handful of results is mostly noise, nothing is called "working" below 20 graded value bets, and past that threshold a simple one-proportion z-test checks whether the win rate is actually distinguishable from break-even before the UI says so. It's a rough gut-check (it assumes bets are independent, which a real season only approximately satisfies), not a rigorous backtest — but it's a real check instead of an assumed "the edge must be real because I flagged it." The bundled sample data seeds a small number of already-graded bets so this section isn't empty on a first run; with that little data it correctly reports "not enough yet" rather than asserting anything. ### Auto-tuning the edge threshold `threshold_tuning.py` uses that same full edge record to test whether a different threshold than the flat 5-point default would actually perform better — sweeping a fine grid, every point from 2pt to 20pt (19 candidates), against every graded edge in history, not just the ones flagged at whatever threshold was active when they were made. For each candidate it computes the same win rate / ROI / significance check as the live backtest above. Testing 19 candidates against the same data raises a real statistical issue on its own: a plain 95%-confidence check (z >= 1.96) on each of 19 candidates will likely find at least one "significant" result by pure chance, the same way 19 coin flips against a fair-coin null usually turn up an outlier. So the significance bar is **Bonferroni-corrected** for the number of candidates tested — the per-candidate significance level is 0.05 / 19 instead of a flat 0.05, which raises the required z-score from 1.96 to roughly 3.0. More candidates tested means a stricter bar to call any one of them significant, which is the correct trade for a finer grid, not an afterthought. **A candidate only ever gets recommended once it clears the same bar the live backtest holds itself to, at this corrected level** — at least 20 graded bets at that threshold AND a Bonferroni-significant edge over break-even. Among candidates that clear both, the one with the best ROI wins. If nothing clears both, `active_threshold()` keeps returning the documented 5-point default rather than picking whichever candidate looks best on a handful of noisy results — the same discipline as the rest of this app, applied to tuning itself instead of just to reporting. Optionally add `API_FOOTBALL_KEY` under the service's Environment tab to go live with real data instead of the bundled sample. **Two free-tier things worth knowing before you rely on this:** - **Cold starts.** Render's free tier spins a service down after periods of no traffic and takes 30-60 seconds to wake back up on the next request — expected on a free plan, not a bug. - **Ephemeral disk.** `predictions_log.json` and `cache/` live on local disk, which free tiers do NOT persist across restarts or redeploys — the track record and value-bet backtest will reset each time the service restarts. Fine for demoing; if you want the track record to actually accumulate over a real season, the fix is swapping `track_record.py`'s flat-file storage for a small free-tier database (Render's free Postgres expires after 90 days; a persistent external store like Supabase's free tier doesn't) — a bigger change than the free hosting step itself, worth doing only once you're confident this is worth running long-term. ## Project layout ``` app.py Flask routes, wires data_source -> fit_parameters -> threshold_tuning -> model -> track_record model.py Poisson goal model + market-odds blending + full edge computation + value-bet detection fit_parameters.py Fits home advantage + team attack/defense from historical results, recency-weighted, with shrinkage threshold_tuning.py Sweeps candidate value-bet edge thresholds (Bonferroni-corrected) against graded history data_source.py SampleDataSource (demo, 5 leagues) and LiveDataSource (API-Football, cached) track_record.py Logs every prediction and every model-vs-market edge, grades them, backtests value-bet ROI templates/, static/ Front end Procfile, render.yaml, .gitignore Deployment config for a free host (Render) predictions_log.json Created at runtime — the public prediction/result/edge log (gitignored, not in the repo) cache/ Created at runtime — cached API-Football responses (gitignored, not in the repo) ``` ## Next steps worth considering -
